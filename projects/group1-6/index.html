Cast in Bronze, Set in Stone — Origins

Built for the Sky

Maharaja Sawai Jai Singh II, dissatisfied with the brass astrolabes of his court, commissioned instruments built to architectural scale. Masons and metalworkers raised the Chakra Yantra as a ring of cast metal, precisely centred on a stone pier and levelled against a plumb line, so that its plane would hold true to the sky for centuries rather than seasons.

Five such observatories rose across northern India — in Delhi, Jaipur, Ujjain, Varanasi and Mathura — each a working laboratory where geometry, metallurgy and observation met.

Pointed at a Fixed Star — Alignment

Set Against the Pole

Before a single reading could be trusted, the instrument had to be aligned. Its central axis was tilted to match the local latitude and fixed on the celestial pole — the still point around which every star appears to wheel through the night. Get this angle wrong by a fraction of a degree, and every measurement taken afterward drifts with it.

Once aligned, the ring's plane matched the plane of the sky's equator: a physical model of an invisible coordinate grid, built from stone and bronze.

Daily Observation

Following Sun, Moon, Planets

Each day, astronomers tracked the shadow cast by the instrument's central gnomon as it crept across the graduated ring. The Sun's shadow revealed its position hour by hour. At night, sighting arms were swung toward the Moon, Venus, Jupiter and Saturn — the five planets visible to the naked eye — and their positions logged against the same fixed scale.

Over months of readings, these observations built almanacs precise enough to predict eclipses and planetary conjunctions.

Reading the Ring — Coordinates

Longitude and Latitude of the Sky

Just as lines of latitude and longitude fix a place on Earth, celestial coordinates fix a point in the sky. The Chakra Yantra's graduated edge measured hour angle — the sky's version of longitude — while a second ring recorded declination, the sky's latitude, measured north or south of the celestial equator.

Together, the two readings pinned a star or planet to a single, repeatable coordinate — the same principle that still underlies modern astronomical catalogues.

Legacy Three Centuries On

Stone Instruments Still Accurate Today

Studies of the Jantar Mantar instruments have found some readings accurate to within a couple of minutes of arc — a remarkable figure for devices with no lenses, no motors and no glass at all. Their scale, rather than being excess, was the technology: a larger ring meant every degree occupied more physical space, and error shrank accordingly.

Where a modern telescope gathers faint light through curved glass and sensors, the Chakra Yantra achieved precision through pure geometry — proof that careful construction can rival delicate optics.

Understanding the Instrument

Science & Construction

History

A Royal Observatory

Jai Singh II built five Jantar Mantar complexes — Delhi, Jaipur, Ujjain, Mathura and Varanasi — between 1724 and 1735, each refining earlier Islamic and Hindu instrument traditions into monumental stone architecture designed purely for reading the sky.

Construction

Geometry as Precision

Rather than small brass fittings prone to flex and drift, the Chakra Yantra used massive masonry rings and axes. Scale itself became the source of accuracy: larger radii meant finer graduations and smaller reading error.

Working Principle

The Sky as a Protractor

A fixed axis parallel to Earth's rotational axis lets the ring rotate in step with the stars. An observer aligns a sighting bar with a body and reads its angular position directly off the graduated scale.

Observation Method

Tracking the Wanderers

Planets were logged night after night as they drifted against the fixed stars. Comparing successive readings revealed retrograde motion, conjunctions, and the timing of eclipses and transits.

Physics

Spherical Trigonometry in Stone

Every yantra is a physical solution to a trigonometric equation — converting an observed angle into declination, right ascension or ecliptic longitude using the geometry of the celestial sphere.
